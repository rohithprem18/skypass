\abstract{Observation planning at a small ground station is split across
unconnected tools that predict passes, judge visibility and leave cloud and
conflicts to the operator. This work examines whether an integrated planner with
learned components increases the number of passes observed under clear sky.
SkyPass carries orbital elements through SGP4 propagation, pass extraction,
visibility scoring, cloud-forecast calibration against reanalysis and exact
conflict resolution by a dynamic program linear in the candidates and the
nightly limit. Two learned components replace hand-built approximations:
segmentation of whole-sky camera images as a sub-hourly cloud nowcast, and
gradient-boosted trees that predict apparent magnitude from pass geometry,
trained on \BrightObs{} measured magnitudes. Pass extraction agrees with an independent implementation to
\SkyfieldTca~s using \CallReduction$\times$ fewer propagator evaluations, and the
scheduler matched exhaustive search on all \SchedTrials~instances. Over
\EvalDays~days, a station required to observe every night gained nothing from
the forecast, whereas one free to choose its nights raised realised yield by
\BudgetGainEV\% (expected value) and \BudgetGainClear\% (clear-sky count), and
calibration raised the gain in every regime. A U-Net and a gradient-boosted
pixel classifier estimated each image's cloud fraction to within \CamGbmFrac{}
percentage points, against \CamTunedFrac{} for the best colour-ratio rule, and the learned photometry cut the magnitude error
for unseen satellites from \BrightMaeFitted{} to \BrightMaeLearned{} mag but did
not transfer to a later period with new object classes and designs. Forecast
value depends on the decision it informs, and the learned components help where
their training data match the deployment.}

\keywords{satellite pass prediction, ground station scheduling, interval scheduling, forecast calibration, cloud segmentation, machine learning}

\section{Introduction}

Small ground stations, such as university laboratories, amateur-radio clubs and
individual optical observers, face a recurring planning problem. Several hundred
satellites are potentially observable and generate thousands of passes in a week,
of which only a few dozen merit the operator's time, and the station attends to
one object at a time.

Existing tools address only parts of this. Pass predictors compute rise and set
times from two-line elements (TLEs) and the SGP4
propagator~\cite{vallado2022fundamentals,acciarini2025sgp4}, some with an
illumination test; a few show a weather indication beside a pass, but to our
knowledge none let the forecast \emph{rank} passes or resolve overlapping
passes.

Weather is the more consequential omission. At the site used as a running
example (Chennai, India, \ang{12.92}\,N), mean observed cloud over optically
viable passes was \CloudChennai\%, under which most geometrically favourable
passes are not observable, yet a predictor ranking by culmination elevation
recommends an overcast night. Once several satellites are tracked their passes
also overlap and a single-aperture station has to choose; the literature treats
that from the operator side, as spacecraft tasking and range
scheduling~\cite{li2023srsp,ou2023drl,wang2021agile}, with constraints a small
station does not have.

This paper shows that the small-station problem admits a substantially simpler
exact formulation, and that the treatment of weather is what improves realised
outcomes. The contributions are: an integrated pipeline accounting for the
passes removed by each physical constraint; a validated pass extractor; exact
capacity-constrained conflict resolution requiring neither a solver nor a
metaheuristic; a weather claim verified against ERA5
reanalysis~\cite{hersbach2020era5}; two negative results, namely that
weather-awareness yields no benefit under a fixed nightly quota and that the
uncalibrated forecast degrades performance there; and two learned components,
a cloud nowcast from whole-sky images and a data-driven photometric model, each
evaluated against classical baselines on identical data.

\section{Related Work}

SGP4, defined in Spacetrack Report No.~3~\cite{hoots1980spacetrack} and
maintained in a revised reference implementation~\cite{vallado2006revisiting},
remains with its two-line element sets the standard basis for rapid position
prediction~\cite{vallado2022fundamentals,acciarini2025sgp4}. Accuracy is limited
by the elements rather than the propagator: errors grow with element-set age, as
measured against on-board GPS~\cite{kovar2022tle}. Whether a satellite is \emph{seen} depends on
illumination and photometry, treated with a conical Earth-shadow
model~\cite{vallado2022fundamentals} and a diffuse-sphere phase
function~\cite{fankhauser2023brightness}; the brightness of large
constellations has meanwhile become a concern for ground-based astronomy in its
own right~\cite{hainaut2026constellations}. Measured satellite magnitudes have
recently become available in bulk through the IAU CPS SCORE
repository~\cite{score2026}, which makes a data-driven photometric model
possible.

Satellite range scheduling is an oversubscribed combinatorial
problem~\cite{li2023srsp}, usually approached with multi-objective optimisation,
metaheuristics or reinforcement learning~\cite{ou2023drl}; for
optical ground stations, a recent proposal is a genetic algorithm with tabu
memory~\cite{zhai2026optical}. Earth observation scheduling is a large adjacent
literature~\cite{wang2021agile}, in which cloud cover appears as an uncertainty
hedged during imaging tasking~\cite{wang2020robust} and recent planners couple
cloud nowcasts with the schedule~\cite{wang2026cloudaware}, although always on
the \emph{operator} side. Where the structure is single-machine interval
selection, exact polynomial algorithms exist: weighted interval scheduling is a
textbook dynamic program~\cite{kleinberg2006algorithm,cormen2022introduction}.

Professional observatories schedule around
weather with queue schedulers that re-plan as conditions
change~\cite{xie2024astrosa}, but assume an institutional software stack and
on-site sky monitoring. Forecast verification and forecast value are mature
fields~\cite{brown2021met}, and statistical post-processing is
essential before a forecast drives a decision~\cite{vannitsem2021postprocessing}.
Forecast value depends on the user's decision problem and not only on
skill~\cite{richardson2000skill,raeth2026decision}. Ground-based sky imaging estimates cloud from
colour: the classical rule thresholds the red-to-blue
ratio~\cite{long2006retrieving}, and convolutional networks now segment cloud in
whole-sky images~\cite{xie2020segcloud}, building on encoder-decoder
architectures such as U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet}. To our knowledge neither
forecast post-processing nor sky imaging has been applied to observer-side pass
planning.

\section{System Design}
\label{sec:design}

SkyPass is a five-stage pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Each stage is a pure
function of the preceding one, so any stage may be ablated independently, which
is the mechanism by which the weather claim is evaluated.

\subsection{Element sets, propagation and pass extraction}
Element sets are fetched from CelesTrak~\cite{celestrak}, checksum-validated,
deduplicated per NORAD identifier keeping the newest epoch, and \emph{archived
under the date of retrieval}, since CelesTrak serves only current elements and
without a local archive a published prediction cannot be repeated. Sets older
than $\tau_\mathrm{max}$ are rejected. Positions come from the reference SGP4
implementation~\cite{vallado2006revisiting}; the TEME vector is rotated to ECEF by Greenwich mean sidereal
time and converted to topocentric azimuth, elevation and range on the WGS-84
ellipsoid. Polar motion is neglected, being far under the SGP4 error budget, and
refraction is reported but not applied to the horizon mask, so results do not
depend on assumed surface conditions.

Because cost is dominated by propagator calls, pass extraction minimises them
(Fig.~\ref{fig:passfind}). Elevation is sampled on a coarse grid to
\emph{bracket} horizon crossings; since
elevation within a pass is smooth and unimodal, a sign change of
$\varepsilon(t) - \varepsilon_\mathrm{mask}$ between adjacent samples brackets
exactly one crossing. Brackets are refined by bisection to \SI{0.1}{\second} and
culmination by golden-section search. The coarse step is $P/240$ bounded to
\SIrange{5}{30}{\second}, so short-period orbits, whose passes are shortest,
are sampled most finely.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{Fig2}
\caption{Pass extraction (illustrative curve). Elevation is sampled every $h$
seconds (black). A sign change of elevation minus the \ang{10} mask between
neighbouring samples (amber) brackets a rise or set, which bisection refines to
\SI{0.1}{\second} (AOS, LOS); golden-section search locates culmination (TCA).}

\subsection{Visibility scoring}
Each pass receives a score $S \in [0,1]$ combining a geometric quality term with
multiplicative gates:
\begin{equation}
S = \underbrace{\left(w_e f_e + w_d f_d\right)}_{\text{geometry}}
    \cdot \; f_m \cdot f_s ,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $f_e = \min(\varepsilon_\mathrm{max}/\ang{60},1)$ and
$f_d = \min(T/\SI{480}{\second},1)$ saturate culmination elevation and duration,
$f_m$ is a photometric factor and $f_s$ a sky-condition factor.

The multiplicative form is deliberate: an eclipsed pass or one in a bright sky
scores zero irrespective of its geometry, and expected cloud scales the score
down in proportion, which an additive score cannot represent. For optical work the object has to be sunlit and the observer in
darkness; Earth shadow uses the dual-cone
construction~\cite{vallado2022fundamentals}, and apparent magnitude follows the
standard diffuse-sphere relation~\cite{fankhauser2023brightness}
\begin{equation}
m = m_\mathrm{std} + 5\log_{10}\!\frac{d}{\SI{1000}{\kilo\metre}}
    - 2.5\log_{10} F(\psi),
\label{eq:mag}
\end{equation}
with $F(\psi) \propto (\pi-\psi)\cos\psi + \sin\psi$ normalised to $F(0)=1$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}). In radio mode the illumination and photometric terms
are dropped.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{Fig3}
\caption{Observation geometry (schematic, not to scale). A pass is observable when
the satellite is sunlit, outside the conical Earth shadow, while the Sun is more
than \ang{6} below the observer's horizon. Its apparent magnitude then depends
on the range $d$ and the phase angle $\psi$ between the directions to the Sun and
to the observer \eqref{eq:mag}; an eclipsed satellite scores zero whatever its
elevation $\varepsilon$.}
\label{fig:geometry}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Weather integration and calibration}
\label{sec:calib}
The sky factor is $f_s = 1 - \hat{c}$, where $\hat{c}$ is the expected cloud
fraction at culmination, interpolated linearly between hourly samples. It is
\emph{not} the raw forecast, which is heavily over-confident as a predictor. We
fit
\begin{equation}
\hat{c} = \mathbb{E}[c \mid c_f] = a + b\,c_f
\label{eq:calib}
\end{equation}
by least squares on a window that \emph{ends before} the period being planned,
restricted to local night hours, and $\hat{c}$ is clipped to $[0,1]$. The slope
$b$ quantifies the signal: $b \approx
1$ means the forecast can be used directly, $b \approx 0$ that the calibrated
value falls back to site climatology. For the threshold value model the planner instead weights
each pass by the calibrated probability of clear sky,
$P(c \le \theta \mid c_f)$, estimated in ten forecast bins on the same window
and shrunk toward the site's base rate with 20 pseudo-observations, so each value
model is paired with the planner that maximises its expectation.

\subsection{Conflict resolution}
\label{sec:sched}
A single-aperture station observes one object at a time and needs a setup gap
$g$ between targets, so choosing the best mutually compatible subset of passes
is weighted interval scheduling. Writing $v_j = S_j \pi_j$ for the value of pass
$j$ (score times priority), indexing passes $1,\dots,n$ by finish time and
letting $p(j)$ be the largest $i<j$ compatible with $j$ ($0$ if none), the
textbook recurrence~\cite{kleinberg2006algorithm,cormen2022introduction} gives
the exact optimum in $O(n\log n)$.

That alone is insufficient: unconstrained, a station observes every candidate
and weather-awareness has nothing to select. Real stations are limited by
operator and instrument time, so a capacity budget of $K$ observations per night
adds a cardinality dimension:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathrm{OPT}[j][k] = \max\bigl(&\mathrm{OPT}[j\!-\!1][k],\\
&v_j + \mathrm{OPT}[p(j)][k\!-\!1]\bigr),
\end{split}
\label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{OPT}[0][k]=\mathrm{OPT}[j][0]=0$. This is exact. The table has one entry for each of the $n$
candidate passes and each count up to the nightly limit $K$, and each entry is
filled in constant time, so after the sort the cost is $O(nK)$: doubling either
the candidates or the limit doubles the work. Observing nights are disjoint,
so applying the budget per night and solving each independently stays exact.
Nights are defined in local solar time, so an evening and the small hours that
follow form one session.

\end{figure}

Two optional models replace hand-built approximations (dashed in
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}); the planner runs unchanged without them.

The cloud forecast is hourly and, as Section~\ref{sec:value} shows, cannot
discriminate within a night; a whole-sky camera can. Two segmenters label each
pixel of a camera image as cloud or sky. The first is a U-Net
\cite{ronneberger2015unet} with three down-sampling stages and 16 base channels
(Fig.~\ref{fig:unet}), trained with a binary cross-entropy masked so that pixels
outside the fisheye contribute nothing, and with rotations by multiples of
\ang{90} and flips, because the sky has no preferred azimuth. The second is a
gradient-boosted classifier~\cite{friedman2001greedy} on fifteen per-pixel
features: the colour channels, the red-to-blue ratio, the normalised blue-red
difference, saturation and brightness, and the local mean and spread of two of
them at two scales. The cloud fraction of the image replaces the calibrated
forecast for the next hour; being a measurement, it is not itself calibrated.

The magnitude gate of \eqref{eq:mag}, with a tabulated standard magnitude, is an
ordering rather than a prediction. The learned alternative, gradient-boosted
regression trees, takes the phase angle, range, elevation, solar elevation and
orbital altitude at culmination, the sphere prediction, the satellite family
recognised from the catalogue name and the observing band; the family is known
for every catalogued object, so the model applies to satellites never
measured.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The catalogue comprises \NCatalogue{} unique objects from CelesTrak GP groups,
checksum-validated and deduplicated, observed from seven stations spanning
distinct cloud climatologies: Chennai and Bengaluru (monsoon), Leh
(high-altitude arid), Tucson (desert), Singapore (equatorial), and Reykjav\'ik
and Svalbard (maritime polar). Unless stated otherwise, the mask is \ang{10}, the
dark-sky threshold \ang{-6} and the setup gap \SI{5}{\minute}. Timing is checked
against a brute-force \SI{1}{\second} dense scan and against
Skyfield~\cite{skyfield}, which shares only the reference SGP4 core with SkyPass. All runs are
single-threaded under Python~\PythonVersion{} with \texttt{sgp4}~\SgpVersion.

All three weather products come from Open-Meteo~\cite{openmeteo}: the
operational forecast, archived past forecasts at lead times of 1--7 days, and
ERA5 reanalysis~\cite{hersbach2020era5} as verification truth. One
methodological point silently invalidates the evaluation if overlooked: the
archive endpoint has to be pinned to the ERA5 model, since at its default it
returns a best-match series which, for recent dates, \emph{is} the archived
forecast, so verifying against it compares a forecast with a copy of itself and
reports a spurious skill of 1.0.

Measured magnitudes come from SCORE~\cite{score2026}, whose rows carry the
SatChecker geometry of each observation. Removing rows flagged as discrepant or
unilluminated, those below the horizon, and the solar sail ACS~3, which alone
supplies most of the repository, leaves \BrightObs{} observations of
\BrightSats{} satellites in \BrightFamilies{} families, cross-validated in five
folds grouped by satellite and split in time at \BrightSplitDate. Sky images are
the \CamImages{} labelled whole-sky images of WSISEG~\cite{xie2020segcloud}, from
one camera, cross-validated in five contiguous blocks because consecutive frames
are near-duplicates. Every method sees identical training data.
The camera baselines are the red-to-blue ratio rule~\cite{long2006retrieving} at
its classical threshold of 0.6 and with the threshold tuned on the training
images; the photometric baselines are the sphere model with its tabulated
standard magnitudes and with one standard magnitude per family fitted on the
training data.

A generative AI assistant (Claude, Anthropic) was used to help edit and
restructure the manuscript text and to write the scripts that build it; the
authors reviewed all such output and take responsibility for the content.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Pass-prediction accuracy and cost}
\label{sec:accuracy}

Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}a compares SkyPass against both references over
\NSats{} objects and \NMatched{} matched passes. Against Skyfield, mean
agreement is \SkyfieldAos~s on acquisition and \SkyfieldTca~s on culmination, and culmination elevation agrees to
\SkyfieldElev\textdegree. The apparent \DenseAos~s ``deviation'' from the
dense scan is not SkyPass error: a \SI{1}{\second} grid locates a crossing only
to within its own step, so it lags the true crossing by a mean of
\SI{0.5}{\second}, and the refined estimator is therefore more accurate than the
brute-force reference it is conventionally compared against. The cost difference
is large: \CallsFast{} propagator evaluations against \CallsDense{}, a factor of
\CallReduction. Detection recall is \Recall\%, the residual being short grazing
transits that peak barely above the mask.

\subsection{Element-set limits on prediction accuracy}
\label{sec:elements}

Sub-second numerical agreement is meaningful only if the elements support that
precision. The median element-set age in the operational catalogue was
\EpochMedian~days (\EpochUnderOne\% under one day). CelesTrak and AMSAT sets
differ by a mean of \ProviderTcaMean~s in culmination over \ProviderN{} matched
passes, but by a median of \ProviderTcaMedian~s: the providers largely
redistribute the same upstream sets, so this is not an independent check. Re-predicting the same passes from progressively
older sets of \AgeObjects{} low-Earth objects gives \AgeComparisons{} aged
comparisons (Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}b). Within a day of epoch the elements contribute \AgeDayOne~s, the
same order as the \SkyfieldTca~s numerical agreement of
Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}, so neither term dominates; degradation is then
sharply super-linear and heavy-tailed, the mean reaching \AgeWeek~s (median
\AgeWeekMedian~s) at 7--14 days and \AgeMonth~s (median \AgeMonthMedian~s) beyond
a month, so a 5--7 day staleness cutoff suits timing-critical work and daily
element refresh is the largest single lever on prediction accuracy.

\subsection{Skill of the cloud forecast}
\label{sec:forecast}

Weather-aware planning is justified only if the forecast is informative at the
lead times over which an observer plans. Archived forecasts were verified
against ERA5 at all seven stations over \ForecastDays{} days, scoring the
operational decision of whether an hour is clear enough to observe at a
\ClearThreshold\% cloud threshold. Because accuracy misleads when clear hours
are rare, we report the Heidke skill score, a chance-corrected measure in wide
use for categorical verification~\cite{ebert2022rare} (Fig.~\ref{fig:skill}).
Skill decays from HSS \HssOne{} at one day to \HssThree{} at three and
\HssSeven{} at seven, beating persistence (\HssPersistence) up to three days
ahead but not from four days on, so a weather-aware planner is best run on a
rolling short horizon. Skill is also
strongly site-dependent, the spread between stations being comparable to the
decay with lead time and lowest at the two monsoon stations: the expected benefit
is a local property rather than a universal constant.

\caption{Skill of the archived cloud forecast against ERA5, pooled over
\SkillStationsWord{} stations. (a) Heidke skill score of the clear-hour decision
by lead time, with persistence and climatology baselines. (b) Mean absolute
error of cloud fraction.}
\label{fig:skill}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Value of weather-aware scheduling}
\label{sec:value}

Five planners select from an identical candidate set, drawn from
\EvalObjects{} objects, at each of \EvalStationsWord{} stations over \EvalDays{}
days (Svalbard has no observable passes), and every timetable is scored against
ERA5. Since the
valuation of a clouded observation is a modelling choice, we report both an
\emph{expected value} model, $\sum_j v_j (1-c_j)$, and a \emph{threshold} model,
$\sum_j v_j \mathbf{1}[c_j \le \theta]$ with $\theta = \ClearThresholdEval\%$.
Two operating rules are compared (Fig.~\ref{fig:rules}): a fixed nightly quota,
and a window budget that lets the station skip nights.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{Fig7}
\caption{The two operating rules, illustrated (not data). Each column is one
night split into evening, midnight and late; cloud tends to persist through a
night. Under a nightly quota the forecast can only reorder passes within a
night; under a window budget it decides which nights to use.}
\label{fig:rules}
\end{figure}

Under a fixed nightly quota the forecast yields no benefit: \QuotaGainEV\% and
\QuotaGainClear\% under the two models, against oracle headroom of
\QuotaOracleEV\% and \QuotaOracleClear\%, and the station-bootstrap intervals
of both gains include zero (\QuotaGainEVLo{} to \QuotaGainEVHi\% and
\QuotaGainClearLo{} to \QuotaGainClearHi\%). The same holds per station: the
oracle shows substantial headroom at every station, while the forecast-driven
planner gains at most a few per cent and loses at three, and no forecast lead
time from one to seven days changes this (Fig.~\ref{fig:leadcal}a). The forecast
is not uninformative: forecast and observed cloud correlate at $r = \PassCorr$
at the candidate-pass timestamps, and the clearest-forecast decile does find
clearer skies, reducing observed cloud by $\CloudReduction$. The limitation is that a quota permits only
\emph{reordering within a night}, which two measured properties defeat: cloud is
autocorrelated over the hours a session spans, the between-night standard
deviation being $\BtwWthRatio\times$ the within-night value, while the
ninetieth-percentile pass carries $\BaseSkew\times$ the mean weather-free value.
The score being a product, selecting for clear sky surrenders base value, an
unfavourable exchange when pass quality is the more variable factor.

Given the same cap plus a window budget, so the station may \emph{skip} a night,
the result reverses (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}): which nights to use is a day-scale
decision, the scale at which the forecast retains skill. We report the central
of the three budget levels as the headline; Fig.~\ref{fig:budget} shows all
four regimes. Observing on \BestBudgetMode{} (\BudgetTotal{} observations),
realised yield improves by
\BudgetGainEV\% and \BudgetGainClear\% (95\% station-bootstrap intervals
\BudgetGainEVLo--\BudgetGainEVHi\% and \BudgetGainClearLo--\BudgetGainClearHi\%),
recovering \BudgetRecoveredEV\% and
\BudgetRecoveredClear\% of the attainable oracle gains; clear-sky passes rise
from \BudgetBlindClearRate\% to \BudgetClearRate\%, and a quarter-of-nights
budget raises the threshold-model gain to \TightGainClear\%
(\TightGainClearLo--\TightGainClearHi\%).

Calibration raises the gain in every regime. Under the quota, supplying
the model cloud field straight to the score costs \QuotaRawEV\% relative to
disregarding weather; under the half budget the raw field still helps
(\RawBudgetEV\% and \RawBudgetClear\%) but less than the calibrated one. Fitted
slopes (Fig.~\ref{fig:leadcal}b) lie between \SlopeMin{} and \SlopeMax{} and intercepts between
\InterceptMin{} and \InterceptMax{}, so clear forecasts are followed by more
cloud than predicted and cloudy ones by less. Calibration \eqref{eq:calib}
shrinks the forecast toward climatology in proportion to its demonstrated
reliability.

Repeating the evaluation with epoch-correct geometry, that is, with the element
set current on each night, raises the threshold-model gain under the
quota from \QuotaGainClear\% to \HistQuotaClear\% and at a quarter budget from
\TightGainClear\% to \HistTightClear\%, but lowers it at the half budget from
\BudgetGainClear\% to \HistBudgetClear\%, the expected-value gain there being
unchanged at \HistBudgetEV\%. Back-propagated geometry therefore does not
inflate the result systematically, although it is not uniformly conservative.

\subsection{Conflict resolution in practice}
\label{sec:schedresults}

On \SchedTrials{} randomised instances small enough to enumerate exhaustively,
the dynamic program matched the optimum in \DPOptimalPct\% of cases. Greedy
selection \emph{by the score itself} attains \GreedyValuePct\% on average, so
the dynamic program buys little in the average case; its advantage is being
exact at negligible cost with no adverse case (Fig.~\ref{fig:sched}a). The
rules an operator applies
fare worse, optimising a proxy rather than the objective: selection by highest
culmination attains \GreedyElevPct\% on average, is optimal in only
\GreedyElevOptPct\% of instances and falls to \GreedyElevMin\% at worst. An
order-based genetic algorithm of the type common in the satellite-tasking
literature (population \GAPopulation, \GAGenerations{} generations) reaches
\GAPct\% of the optimum but needs roughly \GASlowdown$\times$ more time, a ratio
that depends on that search budget; once the observer-side
problem is recognised as interval scheduling, an exact polynomial solution is
available and a metaheuristic is unnecessary. Scheduling is not the bottleneck in
any case, the dynamic program handling \DPBigN{} candidates in \DPBigMs~ms
against the seconds to minutes propagation needs (Fig.~\ref{fig:sched}b).

The funnel is steep and physically interpretable (Fig.~\ref{fig:funnel}): at the

\subsection{Cloud nowcasting from whole-sky images}
\label{sec:camres}

The classical colour-ratio rule at its fixed threshold of 0.6 does not transfer
to this camera: it labels \CamFixedAcc\% of pixels correctly and misestimates
the image cloud fraction by \CamFixedFrac{} percentage points on average, since
the ratio depends on the colour response of the instrument. Tuning the threshold
on the training images (\CamThreshRange) recovers
most of the gap, to \CamTunedAcc\% of pixels and \CamTunedFrac{} points. Both
learned segmenters are better again (Fig.~\ref{fig:cloudcam}). The U-Net reaches
a cloud intersection-over-union of \CamUnetIou{} and the pixel classifier
\CamGbmIou{}, and both estimate the cloud fraction to within \CamGbmFrac{}
points. The pixel classifier was the more accurate segmenter in every fold, plausibly because its
texture features resolve cloud edges that the U-Net, trained at 160 pixels,
smooths; for the cloud fraction the two are equivalent, and SkyPass deploys the
pixel classifier, which needs no deep-learning framework. All images come from
one camera.

\subsection{Learned satellite brightness}
\label{sec:brightres}

For satellites absent from training, the learned model predicts apparent
magnitude with a mean absolute error of \BrightMaeLearned{} mag, against
\BrightMaeFitted{} for the sphere with one fitted standard magnitude per family
and \BrightMaeSphere{} for the sphere with tabulated values
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bright}a), and as a visibility decision at the 6~mag limit its
balanced accuracy is
\BrightVisLearned{} against \BrightVisFitted{} and \BrightVisSphere{}. Orbital
altitude and family carry most of the skill (Fig.~\ref{fig:bright}b), standing in
for size, attitude and mitigation design, which a sphere with one albedo cannot
represent.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Fig13}
\caption{Learned satellite brightness on \BrightObs{} SCORE observations of
\BrightSats{} satellites. (a) Mean absolute magnitude error for satellites absent

The advantage does not survive a change of period. Trained before
\BrightSplitDate{} and tested after, the learned model's error
(\BrightTempMaeLearned{} mag) equals that of the fitted sphere
(\BrightTempMaeFitted{}) and its balanced visibility accuracy falls to
\BrightTempVisLearned{}. It stays better on the \BrightTestOnlyFamilies{}
families absent from training (\BrightTempNewLearned{} against

(\BrightTempEstLearned{} against \BrightTempEstFitted{}); in Starlink, the largest
family, the errors are \BrightStarlinkLearned{} and \BrightStarlinkFitted{} mag,
consistent with design changes between generations. The model interpolates
across satellites but not across time, so the sphere stays the default and the
learned model is an option to be retrained as SCORE grows.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Cloud cover is an imperfect proxy for observing conditions: total cloud
fraction on a reanalysis grid cell of order \SI{25}{\kilo\metre} describes one
line of sight only coarsely, and transparency, seeing, cloud height and aerosol
are not modelled. A camera nowcast can be read from an image with small error
(Section~\ref{sec:camres}), the route to the one regime in which the forecast
gives no benefit; its planning value could not be measured, because public camera
archives do not cover the evaluation nights, and its transfer to other cameras is
untested.

The weather-value gains pool \EvalStationsWord{} stations and \EvalDays{} nights
in one season; with so few clusters the station-bootstrap intervals are
approximate and probably too narrow, and the calibrated-minus-raw difference was
not tested separately.

Neither value model captures partial credit; the scheduler accepts a station's
own value function unchanged. One instrument
observing one object at a time is assumed, so multiple apertures or a station
network would need a different algorithm. The learned photometry drifts as
designs change (Section~\ref{sec:brightres}); the ageing measurement is
single-season and limited to low Earth orbit.

\section{Conclusion}

SkyPass integrates pass prediction, visibility analysis, cloud-forecast
reasoning and exact conflict resolution into one planner for a small ground
station. A forecast is informative about which \emph{nights} merit observation
and much less so within a night, so a planner under a fixed nightly quota gains
nothing, whereas one with a budget to allocate gains \BudgetGainEV\% (expected
value) and \BudgetGainClear\% (clear-sky count); calibration against reanalysis
raises the gain in every regime tested. Forecast value is a property of the
decision it informs, a finding that should carry over to other small facilities,
though only one setting is tested here.

The learned components follow the same principle: segmentation of a whole-sky
image supplies the sub-hourly signal a quota-bound station needs, and learned
photometry predicts magnitudes for unseen satellites far better than the sphere,
but only while its training data describe the satellites in orbit.
